\begin{abstract}
Hate speech detection systems are increasingly deployed to assist human
moderators, yet two gaps limit their practical value: most models give no
faithful account of \emph{why} a post was flagged, and multilingual
code-mixed content---notably Hinglish (Hindi--English)---remains
under-served. We present a four-phase study on English and Hinglish social
media text. First, we establish baselines from TF--IDF classifiers to
multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM-R, MuRIL) on HateXplain. Second, we
LoRA-fine-tune a small instruction-tuned LLM to emit a structured
\emph{label + rationale} pair whose format is machine-checkable. Third, we
optimise this model with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) under a
composite reward $R = \alpha R_{\text{label}} + \beta R_{\text{faith}} -
\gamma R_{\text{bias}}$, where $R_{\text{faith}}$ is a masking-based
faithfulness score that penalises decorative rationales and
$R_{\text{bias}}$ penalises rationales that merely cite identity terms.
Finally, we audit all models with comprehensiveness/sufficiency faithfulness
metrics, subgroup AUC and BPSN/BNSP unintended-bias metrics, and HateCheck
functional tests. Our baseline already shows measurable identity-term bias
(e.g., BPSN of 0.65 for anti-Jewish content), motivating the bias-aware
reward. % TODO: add headline result once Phase 2-3 runs complete.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Online hate speech causes real harm, and human moderation does not scale.
Automated classifiers help, but a bare probability score gives moderators
little to act on and gives researchers little to audit. If a system says a
post is hateful \emph{because of specific words}, both the moderator and the
auditor can check that claim. Meanwhile, moderation demand in India and
similar multilingual markets involves \emph{code-mixed} text---Roman-script
Hindi--English (Hinglish)---where most English-only systems degrade.

This project makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{structured rationale format} for instruction-tuned LLMs
  (\texttt{Label:}/\texttt{Rationale:}) whose parseability, label accuracy
  and rationale faithfulness can each be measured and rewarded
  (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A \textbf{composite reward} for GRPO post-training that combines
  label accuracy with a masking-based \emph{faithfulness} term and an
  identity-term \emph{bias penalty}, with tunable weights
  $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ (\S\ref{sec:reward}).
  \item A \textbf{bias-aware evaluation protocol}: comprehensiveness and
  sufficiency for rationales; subgroup AUC, BPSN and BNSP
  \citep{borkan2019nuanced} per identity group; HateCheck functional tests
  \citep{vidgen2021hatecheck}; and out-of-distribution transfer
  (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Hate speech datasets.} HateXplain \citep{mathew2020hatexplain}
provides English Twitter/Gab posts with three-annotator labels, rationale
spans and target communities---the rationale spans are what make
explainability trainable. HASOC \citep{mandl2020hasoc} covers Hindi,
English and code-mixed tracks. HateCheck \citep{vidgen2021hatecheck}
provides 3{,}728 functional test cases across 29 functionalities, including
identity-term usage in non-hateful contexts---exactly the failure mode our
bias penalty targets.

\paragraph{Rationales and faithfulness.} ERASER \citep{deyoung2020eraser}
established comprehensiveness and sufficiency as the standard faithfulness
measures: removing (keeping) the top-attributed tokens should (almost)
reproduce the prediction. We reuse these both as evaluation metrics and,
novelly, as a \emph{training signal} inside the reward.

\paragraph{Unintended bias.} \citet{borkan2019nuanced} introduced subgroup
AUC, BPSN and BNSP to measure identity-term false positives and missed
in-group hate; we adopt their definitions and report them per target group.

\paragraph{Reward-optimised post-training.} DPO \citep{rafailov2023dpo}
optimises preferences directly; GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} samples a
group of completions per prompt and normalises rewards within the group,
avoiding a separate value model. We use GRPO with a composite,
interpretable reward rather than a learned reward model, so every reward
component can be audited.

\paragraph{Multilingual encoders.} mBERT \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-R
\citep{conneau2020xlmr} and MuRIL \citep{khanuja2021muril} are the standard
multilingual baselines; MuRIL is trained on Indian-language corpora and is
our best candidate for Hinglish. Code-mixing corpora and classifiers
\citep{bohra2018dataset} motivate the Hinglish track.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Unified data schema}
All datasets are normalised to one schema (id, text, binary label,
language, source, rationale mask, target group, split). For HateXplain we
majority-vote the three annotators, resolving ties toward the more harmful
class; hatespeech maps to 1 and offensive/normal to 0---a documented,
deliberate simplification. Rationale masks keep token positions flagged by
at least two annotators; target groups keep communities named by at least
half.

\subsection{Phase 1: baselines}
A TF--IDF + logistic regression classifier (word 1--2-grams; a char
2--5-gram variant for obfuscation) establishes the floor, followed by
fine-tuned mBERT, XLM-R and MuRIL encoders.

\subsection{Phase 2: rationale-generating SFT}
A 1--3B instruction-tuned LLM is LoRA-adapted \citep{hu2022lora} on
HateXplain to output the fixed format
\texttt{Label: <0|1>} + \texttt{Rationale: <short sentence>}. Where
annotator rationales exist (8{,}862 of 15{,}383 training posts) the gold
completion quotes the flagged words; otherwise a template completion is
used. The format is enforced by a parser used identically in training-time
evaluation and in the reward.

\subsection{Phase 3: composite reward}
\label{sec:reward}
Post-training with GRPO maximises
\begin{equation}
R = \alpha\,R_{\text{label}} + \beta\,R_{\text{faith}} - \gamma\,R_{\text{bias}},
\end{equation}
with $\alpha{=}1.0,\ \beta{=}0.5,\ \gamma{=}0.3$ by default (ablated).
$R_{\text{label}} \in \{0,1\}$ scores the parsed label.
$R_{\text{faith}} \in [0,1]$ removes the rationale's content words from the
post and measures $|\Delta P(\text{hateful})|$: rationales whose removal
does not move the model's own prediction are decorative and score near
zero. $R_{\text{bias}} \in [0,1]$ is the fraction of rationale tokens that
are protected-identity terms (lexicon-based; penalised via $-\gamma$), so
the model must cite \emph{toxic content} rather than the mere presence of a
group.

\subsection{Phase 4: evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:eval}
Macro-F1 overall and per language; comprehensiveness/sufficiency for
rationales; subgroup AUC, BPSN, BNSP per target group; HateCheck
per-functionality failure rates; and cross-dataset OOD transfer
(HateXplain$\leftrightarrow$HASOC).

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
\paragraph{Unintended bias exists already at the baseline.} On the TF--IDF
model, BPSN is lowest for \emph{jewish} (0.65) and \emph{african} (0.70)
targets: benign posts merely \emph{mentioning} these groups score nearly as
hateful as genuinely hateful background posts---the identity-term shortcut.
Conversely BNSP is lowest for \emph{refugee} (0.72) and \emph{women}
(0.80): hateful posts targeting them are under-scored. Subgroup AUC ranges
0.71--0.83 (\emph{arab} weakest). This is precisely the failure family that
$-\gamma R_{\text{bias}}$ is designed to penalise.

\paragraph{Faithfulness as a live property.} In our deployment prototype,
every prediction ships with comprehensiveness/sufficiency computed on that
exact input; on clear-cut cases (e.g., violent imperatives) removing the
top-3 attributed tokens flips the prediction almost entirely
(comprehensiveness $\approx 0.93$), while template-like rationales score
near zero---the same signal GRPO optimises.

\section{Ethics}
This research uses datasets containing offensive and hateful content;
exposure is a documented harm and we limit raw-example reproduction in
papers and slides. The system is a decision-\emph{support} tool for human
moderators, not a replacement: annotation of hate speech is subjective
(HateXplain labels are majority votes over three annotators, and our
tie-breaking deliberately resolves toward the harmful class), and automated
decisions can silence legitimate speech, including that of the targeted
communities themselves. We audit identity-term bias explicitly rather than
reporting a single aggregate score. Dataset licences constrain use: our
current models are research artefacts (see the repository's licence
audit); a deployment would require licensed or customer data.

\section{Limitations}
Binary labels flatten a three-way distinction (hateful/offensive/normal);
``offensive'' posts mapping to 0 will look like false negatives to users
who disagree with that choice. Current results are English-only pending
HASOC and Hinglish data. The bias lexicon in $R_{\text{bias}}$ is small and
English-centric, and lexicon-based bias penalties can be gamed; a learned
or multilingual lexicon is future work. The reward model is
interpretable but hand-designed; $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ were set by intuition
pending ablation. Our LLM experiments use 1--3B models under compute
budget constraints; scaling behaviour is untested.
